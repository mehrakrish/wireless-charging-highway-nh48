# Study 1 — Methods, sources and reasoning

This file explains **what was simulated, why, how, and from which sources**, in the order a reviewer would ask.
Every number in the figures and tables can be regenerated by running `python run_study1.py`.

---

## 1. What question Study 1 answers

> For an inductive (wireless) EV charging link, how do the magnetic coupling and the coil-to-coil efficiency
> change as the air gap grows and the car parks or drives off-centre?

This matters for the e-road paper because every later study (dynamic charging on a moving car, Study 3) needs the
coupling `k` at every position of the car over the road coil. Study 1 produces that map and proves it is accurate.

## 2. What kind of simulation this is (and why it is legitimate)

This is **not** a black-box or "made-up curve" simulation. It is a **semi-analytical electromagnetic model**:
the coils are described by their geometry, and textbook physics equations (Maxwell 1873; Grover 1946) give the
inductances exactly for that geometry. The computer only does the summing and the numerical integration.

This approach is standard in the wireless-power literature. The reference paper we validate against
(Ibrahim & Mohammed 2025) uses the same family of equations for its own "analytical" results, next to a
COMSOL finite-element (FEM) model. Analytical models are preferred for design studies because they are fast
(our full 1,271-point grid runs in seconds) and fully transparent: every step is an equation a reviewer can check.

What makes it trustworthy is **validation against independent hardware measurements** (Section 6).

## 4. The physics, step by step

### 4.1 Coil representation
Each flat spiral coil is represented as **N concentric circular turns** with radii

  Rᵢ = R_out − a − p·(i − 1),  i = 1 … N

where `a` is the wire radius (0.95 mm) and `p` the turn pitch.
*Why:* a tightly wound spiral differs from concentric circles only by a tiny radial step per turn; this is the usual
"filament" approximation in WPT coil modelling.

### 4.2 Mutual inductance between two turns, aligned (Maxwell)
For two coaxial circular filaments of radii a and b at axial distance c:

  M = μ₀ √(ab) · [ (2/k − k)·K(k) − (2/k)·E(k) ],  k² = 4ab / ((a + b)² + c²)

K and E are the complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kind.
*Why:* this is the exact closed-form solution of Neumann's formula for two coaxial circles (Maxwell 1873).

### 4.3 Mutual inductance with a sideways offset d (Grover)
For parallel-axis filaments (primary radius Rp, secondary Rs, distance c, offset d):

  M = (μ₀/π) √(Rp·Rs) ∫₀^π [ (1 − (d/Rs)·cos φ) · Ψ(k) / V^{3/2} ] dφ

with α = Rs/Rp, β = c/Rp, V = √(1 + (d/Rs)² − 2(d/Rs)cos φ),
k² = 4αV / ((1 + αV)² + β²), Ψ(k) = (2/k − k)K(k) − (2/k)E(k).
*Why:* Grover's formula (Grover 1946; Akyel, Babic & Mahmoudi 2009) reduces the 2-D Neumann integral to a
1-D integral. We evaluate it with 64-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature.

### 4.4 Self-inductance of a coil
  L = Σᵢ μ₀ Rᵢ (ln(8Rᵢ/a) − 7/4) + Σᵢ≠ⱼ M(Rᵢ, Rⱼ, c = 0)

The first term is the self-inductance of one round-wire turn with **uniform current** (the −7/4 term);
uniform current is correct for Litz wire, whose purpose is exactly to keep current uniform across the conductor.
The second term adds the mutual inductance between all turn pairs of the same coil.

### 4.5 Coupling coefficient
  k = M / √(L1·L2)

### 4.6 Coil-to-coil efficiency, series–series (SS) compensation at resonance
Writing Kirchhoff's voltage law for the two resonant loops (capacitors cancel the coil reactances at 85 kHz):

  η = (ωM)²·R_L / [ (R2 + R_L) · ( R1·(R2 + R_L) + (ωM)² ) ]

Maximising over the load R_L gives R_L,opt = R2 √(1 + k²Q1Q2) and

  η_max = k²Q1Q2 / (1 + √(1 + k²Q1Q2))²,  Q = ωL/R.

*Check built into the code:* the closed form is compared with a brute-force search over 200,001 load values;
they agree to within 10⁻⁹ percentage points.

Two operating cases are reported:
* **Matched load** — the charger re-tunes for every position (upper bound).
* **Fixed load** — tuned once for the design point (aligned, 150 mm), then left unchanged, as a simple
  charger would be.

## 5. How the unpublished turn spacing was obtained

The reference paper does not state the turn pitch of its prototype. We **recover it** by requiring the model's
self-inductance to equal each coil's measured self-inductance (root-finding on p).
* Transmitter: p = 2.004 mm → 0.10 mm between 1.9 mm conductors (close-wound, insulation only).
* Receiver: p = 4.013 mm → 2.11 mm between conductors, consistent with the 2 mm turn distance the paper uses.
* The photograph of the prototype (paper Fig. 16a) shows exactly this: a dense coil and a sparse coil.

**Crucially, only L1 and L2 are used.** The mutual inductance M and coupling k are never used in fitting,
so every coupling comparison in Section 6 is a **blind prediction**.

## 6. Validation

### 6.1 Mathematical check
Grover's single integral is compared with a brute-force 2-D Neumann integral (1,500 × 1,500 points) for three
offset cases: maximum difference 4.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ %. The equations are implemented correctly.

### 6.2 How the measured curves were obtained
The paper's Figures 17 and 20 are **vector graphics** inside the PDF. `tools/extract_curves.py` reads the exact
coordinates of every plotted marker (PyMuPDF), converts them using the axis tick labels (fit residual < 0.0002 in k)
and assigns each to its x-axis label. No reading-by-eye is involved.
*Extraction check:* using the extracted numbers, the authors' own analytical-model errors come out as
4.83 % at 0 mm, 0.56 % at 90 mm and 5.7 % at 130 mm; the paper itself reports ~4.8 %, 0.54 % and 5.68 %.
The extracted 132 mm value (0.1991) also matches their Table 3 (0.198).

### 6.3 Results

| Test | Points | This model, mean / max error | R² | Authors' FEM, mean / max error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M and k at 131 mm (Table 3) | 1 | 4.4 % | — | — |
| k vs distance, 32–312 mm (Fig. 17) | 14 | 3.2 % / 8.3 % | 0.9991 | 2.8 % / 10.2 % |
| k vs offset, 0–130 mm at 100 mm (Fig. 20) | 13 | 3.3 % / 9.7 % | 0.9644 | 3.3 % / 6.1 % |

**Interpretation, stated honestly:** the model is as accurate as the authors' 3-D FEM on average. Up to 90 mm offset
it is within 2.6 %. Beyond 90 mm the measured coupling falls faster than **both** models; at 130 mm our model
over-predicts by 9.7 % and their FEM by 6.1 %. So at large offsets our results are slightly optimistic.

### 6.4 How the remaining model error is handled
Instead of hiding it, every efficiency result is also reported with all k values reduced by the worst validation
error (9.65 %) — a **conservative lower bound**. Across the whole grid this moves the matched-load efficiency by at
most 1.1 percentage points and the fixed-load efficiency by at most 3.4 points. The conclusions do not change.

## 7. Coil quality factor Q (no longer an assumption)

Q measures how low-loss a coil is: Q = ωL / R_ac. It was derived, not assumed:

1. **DC resistance** from the published wire: R_dc = ρ·(wire length)/(180 × π·(0.051 mm)²).
   Wire length = sum of turn circumferences: 42.41 m (transmitter), 12.37 m (receiver).
   → R_dc = 495.9 mΩ and 144.7 mΩ.
2. **AC/DC factor F_R** — at 85 kHz the resistance is higher than at DC (proximity effect). The paper reports a
   measured copper loss of 7.0 W at 2.25 A and 4.92 A in its 200 W test, so
   F_R = 7.0 / (2.25²·R1_dc + 4.92²·R2_dc) = **1.164**.
3. **Q = ωL/(F_R·R_dc)** → **Q1 = 735 (road coil), Q2 = 257 (car coil).**

*Assumption to state:* the 200 W test used the same windings with ferrite added (its inductances, 1103 µH and
114 µH, are both ~40 % higher than the coreless 795 µH and 81 µH, as expected from adding ferrite to the same coils).
Because this calibration has an assumption, F_R is varied from 1.0 to 3.0 in the sensitivity study (Section 9).

## 9. Sensitivity checks (why each one)

Reference point: 175 mm gap, 100 mm offset (middle of the range); baseline efficiency 96.1 %.

| Parameter varied | Range | Effect on efficiency | Why tested |
|---|---|---|---|
| AC/DC resistance factor F_R | 1.0 – 3.0 | +0.6 to −5.9 points | Q is calibrated with an assumption (§7) |
| Coupling k | ± worst validation error (9.65 %) | −0.4 to +0.3 points | Residual model error (§6.3) |
| Coil size (all dimensions scaled) | 400 – 700 mm | +0 to +2.1 points | Real EV pads are often larger than 400 mm |

**Finding:** coil losses (Q) influence efficiency far more than the remaining modelling error does.

## 10. Limitations (must appear in the paper)

1. **Air-core coils only:** no ferrite, aluminium shield or vehicle chassis. Commercial EV pads use ferrite,
   which raises k; our numbers are therefore representative of coreless designs, and conservative in coupling.
2. **Coil-to-coil efficiency only:** inverter, rectifier and ferrite losses are not included. System (DC-to-DC)
   efficiency will be lower. (The reference prototype measured 89.45 % including electronics and core losses.)
3. **Concentric-circle approximation** of the spiral; filament (thin-wire) mutual inductance.
4. **Static positions:** coupling at fixed positions; motion effects are handled in Study 3.
5. **One validation prototype:** results are validated for 400 mm coreless spirals; the size sensitivity
   (500–700 mm) is an extrapolation of the same validated physics, not separately measured.
6. **Q scaling:** in the coil-size sensitivity, Q is held fixed. Scaling every dimension by λ gives L ∝ λ and
   R_dc ∝ λ/λ² = 1/λ, so Q actually grows roughly as λ²; holding it fixed is conservative.

## 11. What Study 1 does and does not claim

* **Does not claim** novelty in the coil model itself — these equations are classical. Study 1 is the **validated
  foundation** of the paper.
* **Does provide:** a validated, fully reproducible coupling-and-efficiency map across the SAE J2954 gap range, a
  physically derived (not assumed) coil Q, explicit error bounds, and the quantified penalty of a fixed-tuned
  charger under misalignment (up to 10.1 points at 250 mm gap / 200 mm offset).
* The paper's novelty must come from the **combined e-road analysis** (Study 1 + piezo energy budget in Study 2 +
  moving-vehicle energy in Study 3).
